\section{Datasets}

This work draws from two main sources of input: 
measured mass spectra with their corresponding molecular structures as SMILES strings 
and a set of known fragmentation rules for the ionization method of interest.

\subsection{Public Databases}

For this work, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) database for EI GC-MS 
was selected as the primary data source for the measured mass spectra. 
NIST is considered the gold standard in the field due to several key advantages: \cite[p.~3]{Wallace2023}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Comprehensive coverage of compounds
    \item Standardized data format ensuring consistency across entries
    \item Spectra experimentally measured under controlled, standardized instrument conditions 
\end{itemize}

Alternative databases such as MassBank, PubChem, HMDB, and MoNa were considered but not utilized in this study.

\subsection{Custom Dataset Construction}

\section{Preprocessing - Generating the Synthetic Data}

Synthetic training data are generated with \texttt{mod} derivation graphs (\Cref{fig:data-gen-overview}).
In this representation, nodes correspond to molecular states (fragments),
while edges represent individual fragmentation transformations.
The resulting graph captures the reaction network and preserves fragment genealogy,
which allows us to trace parent-child relationships and identify reachable fragments.
This information is later used to validate predicted fragment assignments against chemically plausible pathways.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \input{fig-tikz/dataGenOverview.tikz.tex}
    \caption{Overview of the data generation process using \texttt{mod} derivation graphs.}
    \label{fig:data-gen-overview}
\end{figure}

The \texttt{mod} strategy framework provides a systematic approach to explore chemical space 
by guiding fragmentation pathways through both physico-chemical properties 
and experimental data, such as mass spectra. 
A key advantage of this framework is its automatic generation of hypergraphs (reaction networks).
This automation enables the construction of detailed atom flow networks for complex fragmentation patterns, 
making it feasible to track chemical transformations and validate fragmentation predictions at scale.
\cite[p.~7]{Andersen2017}

\subsection{Fragmentation Rule Application Strategies}

The strategy layer accepts fragmentation rules as input and 
utilizes them to construct the corresponding reaction network by applying them to molecular structures.
\Cref{lst:sampelRule} shows one representative rule definition used in this workflow.

    s =
    "[C]1[C]2[C]3([C]4)[_A+.]5"
    ">>"
    "[C.]1" "." "[C]2[C]3([C]4){=}[_A+.]5",

    "radical induced (alpha-)cleavage for a saturated site"

    \label{lst:sampelRule}
\end{listing}

In the \texttt{mod} framework, predicates are callback-based filters evaluated during rule application.
They remove candidate sites that violate chemical constraints encoded by the rule extension
(see \Cref{lst:sampelRule}, line 9).
The decision flow of this filtering step is summarized in \Cref{fig:predicate-filter}.



\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \input{fig-tikz/predicate-filter.tikz.tex}
    \caption{Flow diagram explaining predicate filter application at the fragmentation process.}
    \label{fig:predicate-filter}
\end{figure}

To operationalize these chemical constraints in the filtering stage, three predicate checks are applied:

\paragraph{Heteroatom Check}
In order to ensure chemical plausibility during the application of the rule, 
the heteroatom check retains exclusively those candidate sites 
which contain at least one non-carbon, non-hydrogen atom within their local neighborhood.

The heteroatom check evaluates the neighborhood of the rule-extension location via local graph traversal.
A candidate is accepted only if at least one non-carbon, non-hydrogen atom is found,
formulated as $\mathrm{BFS}\setminus\{\mathrm{H},\mathrm{C}\}\neq\varnothing$.
If the explored neighborhood contains only carbon and hydrogen,
the candidate is rejected.
\Cref{fig:heteroatom-check} contrasts both cases: a valid neighborhood containing a heteroatom
and an invalid neighborhood without one.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \input{fig-tikz/predicate-heteroatom.tikz.tex}
    \caption{Heteroatom check predicate: 
    valid case with detected heteroatom (left) versus invalid C/H-only neighborhood (right).}
    \label{fig:heteroatom-check}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Saturation Check}
The saturation check is formulated as a constrained path search between a start and an end atom.
A candidate path is accepted only if it follows a carbon backbone through single C--C bonds,
while side branches are limited to hydrogen.
This removes candidates that include incompatible branch chemistry,
such as additional carbon side branches.
\Cref{fig:saturation-check} highlights the accepted saturated path in green
and marks a rejected carbon branch.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \input{fig-tikz/predicate-saturation.tikz.tex}
    \caption{Saturation check predicate as constrained path traversal: 
    accepted carbon single-bond path (green) and rejected carbon side branch.}
    \label{fig:saturation-check}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Alkyl Check}
The alkyl check ensures that the fragmentation site is part of an alkyl group, 
restricting fragmentation to aliphatic carbon chains, where such reactions are most likely to occur. 
This constraint helps filter out fragmentation events in 
aromatic rings or other structural contexts where different chemical reactivity dominates. 

\Cref{fig:alkyl-check} visualizes the alkyl check as a graph-constrained traversal: 
a valid path must connect start and end atoms through only single C-C bonds in a carbon backbone, 
while side branches are restricted to hydrogen. 
The green route, which is highlighted, satisfies these constraints; 
however, the carbon branch, which is marked, is rejected.

\section{Latent Representation Schemes}
\subsubsection{Overview}

In this work, we encode molecular fragmentation data through three tightly coupled representation streams:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Molecular states} as chemistry-aware graphs
    \item \textbf{Derivation dynamics} as a hypergraph over molecular states and transformation rules
    \item \textbf{Mass spectra} as peak-intensity vectors aligned with structure-derived latent states
\end{enumerate}

The core design principle is a two-level encoding pipeline:
first, each molecular state is encoded at atom and bond level;
second, these state-level encodings are used as node features in a derivation hypergraph.
The model enables the preservation of local chemical structure, 
while explicitly representing multi-step fragmentation logic.

In particular, a molecule is not encoded as a SMILES sequence, fingerprint, or direct spectrum-oriented tokenization.
Instead, it is represented as an explicit graph object derived from the M\O D framework,
then mapped into a dense latent embedding,
and finally placed into a derivation-hypergraph context together with learned rule representations.

\subsection{Molecular Graph Encoding}

PyTorch Geometric (PyG) \cite{Fey2025} is a library that extends PyTorch to enable efficient processing of graph-structured data.
It provides specialized data structures and operations optimized for graph neural networks (GNNs).

For molecular graph encoding, each molecular state is converted by \texttt{GraphFeaturizerMOD} into a PyG \texttt{Data} object.
Atoms are represented as nodes and bonds as edges,
allowing GNN architectures to learn from both node features (atomic properties)
and edge features (bond information).

Node features are deliberately minimal and chemistry-focused.
For each atom, the feature vector contains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item atom identity (\texttt{atom\_id})
    \item formal charge
    \item radical state
\end{itemize}
Hence, the node representation is
\[
x_i = [\texttt{atom\_id},\; \texttt{charge},\; \texttt{radical}].
\]

Edge features encode bond type as one scalar value,
with single, double, triple, and aromatic bonds mapped to $1.0$, $2.0$, $3.0$, and $1.5$, respectively.
Connectivity is stored in \texttt{edge\_index} in COO format.

Before featurization, preprocessing attempts to detect term-graph inputs 
and converts them into standard graph form where needed.
The objective of the normalization step is to ensure 
the encoder receives a consistently formatted molecular graph representation.

The library handles common challenges in graph processing, 
such as varying graph sizes and irregular connectivity patterns, 
through batching mechanisms that stack multiple graphs into a single tensor representation.

%PyG's core strength lies in its message-passing framework, which enables efficient computation of graph convolutions.
%In each layer, nodes aggregate information from their neighbors through learned transformations, 
%allowing the network to capture hierarchical structural patterns relevant to fragmentation behavior.
%Additionally, PyG provides pre-implemented layers for various GNN architectures (GraphConvolution, GraphAttention, etc.) 
%and inherent permutation invariance, ensuring that the model is robust to different node orderings.

For this work, we utilize PyG's graph convolutional layers to embed molecular structures into a latent space 
that captures chemical properties while remaining efficient to compute.
The flexibility of PyG's API allows seamless integration 
with standard PyTorch components for downstream prediction tasks.


\subsection{Two-Level Molecular Latent Representation}

After graph featurization, each molecular \texttt{Data} object is passed to a learned graph encoder.
In \texttt{DGHypergraphFeaturizer}, this is implemented in \texttt{\_encode\_graphs},
where batches of molecular graphs are processed by \texttt{self.graph\_encoder}.
The output is a dense latent vector of dimension \texttt{dim\_h} per molecular graph.
These embeddings are stored as \texttt{mol\_rep}.

The central architectural distinction is that molecules are not treated as isolated instances.
Each molecular state corresponds to a vertex in a derivation graph (\texttt{mod.DG}),
and this derivation graph is transformed into a hypergraph representation.
The method \texttt{\_collect\_graphs} iterates over derivation-graph vertices, extracts the corresponding molecular graph,
and maps each graph to a contiguous vertex index.
Each state graph is encoded, producing one latent embedding per derivation-graph vertex.
Consequently, node features in the final hypergraph are not raw atom features,
but learned embeddings of complete molecular states.

This yields two explicit representational levels:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Lower level: each molecule is a molecular graph (atoms and bonds).
    \item Higher level: each molecular state becomes a node in a derivation hypergraph.
\end{enumerate}

Technically, molecule encoding proceeds as follows:
first chemistry-aware graph featurization,
then graph-to-vector compression into a dense latent embedding,
and finally use of this embedding as the node feature in the derivation hypergraph.

In the final \texttt{HyperGraphData} object generated from the derivation graph,
\texttt{x} stores molecular embeddings (\texttt{mol\_rep}),
\texttt{edge\_index} stores incidence pairs $(\texttt{vertex\_id},\texttt{hyperedge\_id})$,
and \texttt{edge\_attr} stores learned rule embeddings associated with hyperedges.
Thus, \texttt{x} can be interpreted as per-vertex graph embeddings,
\texttt{edge\_attr} as per-hyperedge rule embeddings,
and \texttt{edge\_index} as the vertex-hyperedge incidence structure.

\subsection{Rule Representation and Hyperedge Annotation}

Rule processing is implemented separately from molecule processing.
Rules are featurized by \texttt{RuleFeaturizerMOD}, but unlike molecular states,
a rule is not expanded into a chemically rich multi-node graph.
Instead, each rule is represented compactly as a one-node \texttt{Data} object,
whose single node feature is the indexed rule ID.

Within \texttt{DGHypergraphFeaturizer}, rule handling is split into three stages.
Rules are first collected in \texttt{\_prepare\_rules}.
They are then encoded in \texttt{\_encode\_rules}.
Finally, they are aggregated in \texttt{\_pool\_rules}.
If multiple rules are associated with one hyperedge,
their embeddings are mean-pooled into one hyperedge-level vector.
As a result, each hyperedge is annotated with a learned representation
of the transformation rule (or rule set) that produced that derivation step.

\subsection{Spectral Vector and Embedding Representation}
Each mass spectrum is treated as a set of tuples $(m/z, I)$, 
where $m/z$ is the mass-to-charge ratio and $I$ is the corresponding peak intensity.
For model input, these tuples are converted into peak-intensity vectors over $m/z$ bins.
These vectors are embedded into a spectral latent space,
which is trained to be compatible with the structure-derived latent representation.
The utilization of a shared latent view facilitates the integration of 
fragmentation topology, molecular-state embeddings, and observed spectral evidence in downstream learning tasks.

\subsection{Disadvantages}
Despite these representational advantages, the proposed encoding pipeline 
also introduces several methodological and practical limitations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Loss of information due to dimensionality reduction from molecular graphs to fixed-size latent vectors
    \item Limited interpretability of latent dimensions and pooled rule embeddings
    \item Potential propagation of preprocessing artifacts into learned representations
    \item Risk of over-squashing and long-range dependency loss in message-passing encoders
\end{itemize}



\section{Model Architecture}

\subsection{Overview of the Learning Framework}

The implemented learning framework is a bidirectional model that couples molecular structure, 
fragment-level information, and mass spectra in a shared latent space. 
As illustrated in \Cref{fig:ml-architecture-diagram}, the model operates on three synchronized inputs: 
a molecular graph representing the precursor structure, 
a fragment-derived representation encoding the fragmentation space associated with that molecule, 
and a binned mass spectrum. 
These three views are processed by dedicated encoders 
and are then linked through a shared latent representation 
that supports both forward spectrum prediction and backward structure retrieval.

On the molecular side, the precursor is represented as a graph and embedded by a graph neural network. 
On the fragment side, the model uses a derivation-graph backend, 
in which fragment graphs and their parent--child relations are encoded explicitly. 
On the spectral side, the measured spectrum is represented 
as a fixed-length vector obtained by binning intensities over the m/z axis 
and is projected into the same latent space through a dedicated spectrum encoder. 
Therefore, the resulting architecture combines a chemically grounded graph representation 
with a vector-based spectral representation in a single trainable framework.

A central design decision is that the model is not a direct spectrum-to-SMILES generator. 
Rather, it is a bidirectional embedding model. 
The forward direction predicts spectra from molecular and fragment information, 
while the backward direction maps spectra into a latent space 
in which candidate molecular structures can be retrieved efficiently. 
This distinction is important for interpreting \Cref{fig:ml-architecture-diagram}: 
the right-hand side of the figure should not be read as a decoder that explicitly reconstructs a molecular graph, 
but as a retrieval-oriented inverse path that aligns spectral embeddings with molecular embeddings.

\subsection{Forward Model: Structure-to-Spectrum Prediction}

The forward branch corresponds to the left-to-right pathway in \Cref{fig:ml-architecture-diagram}. 
Its purpose is to predict a mass spectrum from a molecular structure 
together with fragment-level information derived from precomputed derivation graphs. 
The molecular encoder, \texttt{EncMol}, is implemented as a two-layer graph convolutional network. 
It applies graph convolutions with dimensions $3 \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow d_L$ 
followed by global mean pooling, thereby mapping the input molecular graph to a latent vector $z_{\mathrm{mol}}$. 
This matches the molecular branch depicted in the figure.

The fragment branch is conceptually represented by 
the \emph{Derivation Graph} and \emph{FragTreeEnc} boxes in \Cref{fig:ml-architecture-diagram}. 
In the implementation, this branch is realized by the wrapper module \texttt{FragSetEncoderWrapper}, 
which encodes derivation-graph structure explicitly. 
For each molecule, the dataset constructs fragment graphs and an explicit derivation 
graph whose nodes correspond to fragments and whose directed edges encode fragmentation relations. 
The fragment encoder first embeds the individual fragment graphs using the molecular graph encoder as a backbone. 
Fragment masses are appended as additional node features, 
and a second graph neural network is then applied over the derivation graph itself. 
A global pooling step yields a single latent fragment representation $z_{\mathrm{frag}}$. 
Accordingly, this branch is implemented as a graph encoder over derivation graphs,
not as a simple pooling operation over an unordered fragment bag.

The latent fusion step shown in \Cref{fig:ml-architecture-diagram} as $z_{\mathrm{combined}}$ 
is implemented by a simple arithmetic average of the molecular and fragment latents,
$$
z_{\mathrm{combined}} = \frac{z_{\mathrm{mol}} + z_{\mathrm{frag}}}{2}.
$$
This fused representation is then passed through the forward task head, \texttt{$\text{Head}_{\mathrm{FWD}}$}, 
which projects the latent vector into a task-specific subspace. 
Finally, the shared decoder \texttt{DecSpecLatent} maps this representation to 
a predicted spectrum $\hat{s}_{\mathrm{pred}}$. 
The forward branch is trained with the spectrum decoder as its decoder.

\subsection{Backward Model: Spectrum-to-Structure Reconstruction}

The backward branch corresponds to the spectrum-side pathway in \Cref{fig:ml-architecture-diagram}. 
Its function is to encode spectra into a latent representation that can be aligned with molecular embeddings 
and used for later retrieval. The spectrum encoder, \texttt{EncSpec}, 
receives a fixed-length vector of binned intensities. 
This encoder is implemented as a regularized stack of residual MLP blocks with normalization and dropout. 
This makes the spectral branch deeper and more expressive than the simplified notation in the figure might suggest.

Given an input spectrum, \texttt{EncSpec} produces a latent vector $z_{\mathrm{spec}}$, 
which is then passed through the backward task head, \texttt{$\text{Head}_{\mathrm{BWD}}$}.
This head serves two purposes. First, it provides a task-specific projection 
for latent alignment with molecular embeddings during training. 
Second, it defines the representation that is used at test time for inverse retrieval. 
The projected spectrum embedding is compared to a latent index of molecular embeddings, 
allowing the model to retrieve the most similar candidate structures in embedding space.

It is worth emphasizing that the backward model is not a direct structure decoder in the generative sense. 
Instead, it is a spectrum-to-latent-to-candidate pipeline. 
The model first retrieves candidate molecules by cosine similarity in latent space 
and then optionally re-ranks them by passing each candidate through the forward branch 
and comparing the reconstructed spectrum with the query spectrum. 
Consequently, the ``reconstruction'' in this subsection should be interpreted as 
a process of structure retrieval guided by shared embeddings and forward-model consistency, 
rather than as the explicit generation of a graph.

\subsection{Bidirectionllity}

The bidirectional nature of the framework arises from the fact that molecular graphs 
and spectra are not learned independently. 
As shown in \Cref{fig:ml-architecture-diagram}, both modalities are mapped into related latent spaces 
and are connected by shared components. 
The most important of these are the task heads and the spectrum decoder. 
The forward pathway employs a combination of molecular and fragment representations to predict spectra. 
In contrast, the backward pathway utilizes spectral embeddings 
to align against molecular embeddings and to facilitate retrieval.
Because both directions are trained jointly within the same architecture, 
information learned in one direction constrains the other.

This bidirectionality is also strengthened by the retrieval strategy. 
The molecular encoder is used to build an index over known candidate structures, 
while the spectrum encoder provides the query representation. 
The result is that forward prediction and inverse retrieval are coupled tasks 
of the two views of the same embedding problem. 
In addition, the inverse direction can benefit from the forward model through reranking, 
since candidate molecules are rescored according to how well their forward-predicted 
spectra match the observed spectrum. 
In this sense, the model closes the loop between both directions even though it does not implement a 
fully generative cycle from spectrum directly back to a reconstructed molecular graph.

\subsection{Loss shaping}

The training objective is deliberately multi-component and is best understood 
as a form of loss shaping that stabilizes learning and injects chemically meaningful constraints. 
For the forward branch, the primary reconstruction objective combines cosine similarity 
with a one-dimensional Wasserstein distance between predicted and true spectra. 
The cosine term emphasizes agreement in spectral shape, 
while the Wasserstein term additionally penalizes positional mismatch along the ordered m/z axis. 
The combination is more informative than cosine similarity alone 
and aligns well with the loss panel outlined in \Cref{fig:ml-architecture-diagram}.

A second important component is the precursor-mass constraint. 
This forbidden-region penalty acts as a chemically motivated regularizer 
that penalizes predicted intensity above the precursor mass and suppresses implausible peaks. 
The forward phase also includes a cycle-style consistency term: 
a predicted spectrum is re-encoded by the spectrum encoder 
and encouraged to remain close to the molecular latent representation. 
In addition, a diversity loss discourages collapse toward overly similar spectral predictions within a batch.

For the backward branch, latent alignment is driven by an InfoNCE contrastive objective 
between spectrum and molecule embeddings. 
This enforces that matching molecule--spectrum pairs are close in latent space 
while non-matching pairs are separated. The backward phase further includes spectrum reconstruction 
from the spectral latent, the same precursor-mass penalty, additional cycle-consistency terms, 
and an explicit retrieval-ranking loss that rewards within-batch discrimination of the correct molecular candidate. 
Finally, selected losses are uncertainty-weighted through learned log-variance parameters in the task-head module.

Taken together, these terms show that the architecture in \Cref{fig:ml-architecture-diagram} 
should not be interpreted as a simple encoder--decoder stack. 
Rather, it is a coupled bidirectional system in which 
reconstruction, alignment, ranking, and chemical plausibility constraints are optimized simultaneously. 
This loss shaping is essential for turning the shared latent space into a representation 
that is useful both for forward spectrum prediction and for backward structure retrieval.
