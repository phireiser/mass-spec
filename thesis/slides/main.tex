\section{Problem}

\begin{frame}{What is my thesis about?}
    M \ldots Molecular structure \quad
    S \ldots Mass spectrum
    \begin{itemize}
%      \item \textbf{Computational mass spectrometry} with a focus on \textbf{MS spectrum modeling}
        \item Formulated mathematically as a function:
            \[
                f : M \to S
            \]
        \item Investigation of the \textbf{inverse problem}:
            \[
                f^{-1} : S \to M
            \]
        \item Integration of \textbf{chemically studied reaction rules} modeled in \textbf{M\O D}
        \item Use of fragmentation trees to support traditional machine-learning-based MS models
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\note{
    So what is this project actually about?

    The starting point is quite simple. I treat mass-spectrometry
    modelling as a function: you put in a molecular structure, and you
    get out a mass spectrum. That's the forward direction.

    What I'm really after, though, is the inverse problem --- you have a
    spectrum, and you want to get back to the structure.

    And the idea I'm exploring is to bring the relationships between the
    fragments into that.

    I model those with M\O D, and then use them to help the
    machine-learning models along.
}

\section{Background}

\begin{frame}{Motivation}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{
        \input{../shared/figures/motivation.tikz.tex}
    }
    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\footnotesize Acquisition fast, interpretation slow $\Rightarrow$ the
    bottleneck.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    But why bother modelling this at all?
    What's the motivation?

    Mass spectrometry is one of the most widely used tools we have for
    analysing molecules. But reading that data is still mostly done by hand, by experts, and it takes time.

    So we can acquire spectra very fast --- and none of that speed
    carries over into the analysis. That's where the bottleneck sits.

    And the hope is: if we feed chemical structure into the models, they
    get more interpretable and they generalise better.
}

\note{
    A quick reminder of how a mass spectrometer works.

    We start with a sample, and that sample gets ionised.

    The ions are then separated by their mass-to-charge ratio,

    and at the end they hit a detector
    --- and that's what gives us the spectrum.

    The part that matters for us:
    under electron ionisation the molecule doesn't stay in one piece, it breaks apart.
    So the spectrum is really a fingerprint of those fragments.
    And that's exactly what I exploit later on.
}

\begin{frame}{Data Generation Overview}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.75\textwidth}{!}{
        \import{../shared/figures/}{dataGenOverview.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    This is the whole pipeline in one picture.

    I take a molecule and fragment it with the rules in M\O D.

    That gives me a fragmentation graph,

    and from that graph I read off the fragments.

    Then I go the other way round: I take those fragments and put them
    back together, using the inverse of the reaction rules.

    And that's how I get candidate structures for a given spectrum.
}

\begin{frame}{M\O D - Predicate}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-filter.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    M\O D lets you hook in so-called predicates. They're callbacks that
    get called during the transformation,

    and I use them to filter candidates according to the rule extension.

    On the next few slides I'll walk you through the ones I implemented.
}

\begin{frame}{M\O D - Predicate - Alkyl Group}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{%
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-alkyl.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    The first one is the alkyl group. An alkyl substituent contains
    nothing but carbon and hydrogen.

    So I start where the rule is extended, and collect the neighbours
    breadth-first, until I run into atoms that are already part of the
    match of the original rule.

    And then I simply check: is everything I collected a carbon or a
    hydrogen? If yes, it's a valid alkyl group. If not, I reject it.
}

\begin{frame}{M\O D - Predicate - Heteroatom Group}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{%
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-heteroatom.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    The heteroatom group is basically the mirror image of that. Here I
    need at least one atom that is \emph{not} a carbon or a hydrogen.

    Again I collect the neighbours breadth-first --- the ones the original
    rule hasn't matched ---

    and then I check whether that set contains anything outside hydrogen
    and carbon.

    If it does, it's a valid heteroatom group. If it's only hydrogen and
    carbon, it gets rejected.
}

\begin{frame}{M\O D - Predicate - Saturation}
    \vspace{1.5em}
    \centering
    \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-saturation.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    And the third one is saturation. Here I'm looking for a chain of
    carbons that are connected only by single bonds, and where every
    carbon carries nothing but hydrogens on the side.

    So I search from the start atom to the end atom, along carbon--carbon
    single bonds. The example at the top passes. The one at the bottom
    fails, and there are two reasons for that: the branching carbon, and
    the double bond.
}

\note{
    This is the model I implemented. It takes three inputs: the molecule
    graph, the derivation graph --- so the fragmentation --- and the mass
    spectrum.

    Each of them gets its own encoder, and they all map into one shared
    latent space. For the molecule that's a GCN with global pooling, for
    the derivation graph a two-level graph encoder, and for the spectrum a
    residual MLP.

    Going forward, I fuse the molecule and the fragment latents, and
    decode them into a predicted spectrum.

    The backward direction deserves a comment, because it is \emph{not} a
    spectrum-to-graph decoder. It's a retrieval path. I align the spectrum
    embedding with the molecular embeddings, so at test time I can pull
    out the nearest candidate structures by cosine similarity, and then
    rerank them with the forward model.

    Both directions share the task heads and the decoder, and they're tied
    together by two auxiliary losses: cycle consistency, and an InfoNCE
    contrastive alignment. So forward prediction and inverse retrieval end
    up constraining the very same latent space.
}

\section{Results}
\begin{frame}{Results -- Bidirectional Retrieval}
    {\footnotesize Proof-of-concept $\cdot$ ${\sim}100$ EI compounds
    $\cdot$ $22$ test queries}
    \vskip 0.4em
    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
      \column{0.4\textwidth}
        \centering
        \begin{tabular}{ l r }
          \hline
          Metric & Full model \\
          \hline
          Recall@1  & $0.091$ \\
          Recall@5  & $0.318$ \\
          Recall@10 & $0.500$ \\
          Recall@20 & $0.864$ \\
          MRR       & $0.243$ \\
          \hline
        \end{tabular}
      \column{0.58\textwidth}
        \centering
        \figorpending{width=\linewidth}{../shared/figures/recall_at_k.pdf}
    \end{columns}
    \vskip 0.3em
    \centering
    {\footnotesize $\Rightarrow$ candidate narrowing, not single-shot ID}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Now to the results. And let me say up front that this is a proof of
    concept: roughly a hundred EI compounds, with 22 held-out test
    queries.

    Recall at k is the fraction of queries where the true structure shows
    up among the top k retrieved candidates.

    For the full model, Recall@1 is about 0.09, Recall@5 about 0.32, at 10
    we're at 0.50, at 20 at 0.86, and the mean reciprocal rank is 0.24.

    So getting it right on the first guess is hard. But the correct
    molecule usually does get pulled up towards the top of the list. In
    other words, this behaves as a tool that narrows down candidates ---
    which is still genuinely useful in a structure-elucidation workflow.
    It's just not a single-shot identifier.

    One caveat on these numbers: the test set is small. With 22 queries,
    recall moves in steps of about 0.045 --- so I'm careful about reading
    too much into the differences between variants.
}

\begin{frame}{Results -- What Actually Helps? (Ablation)}
    \centering
    \figorpending{width=0.58\linewidth}{../shared/figures/cosine_mrr.pdf}
    \vskip 0.2em
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Forward cosine ${\approx}0.43$ --- flat across ablations
      \item \textbf{Reranking}: only significant gain
            (MRR $0.24 \to 0.10$, $p \approx 0.013$)
      \item Fragment branch: no significant benefit yet
    \end{itemize}}
\end{frame}
\note{
    To find out what actually drives that, I ran an ablation against the
    full model: once without the fragments, once without the derivation
    graph, and once without reranking.

    First the forward task. All the variants land at almost the same
    cosine similarity, around 0.43. So the forward reconstruction barely
    notices the ablations at all.

    Second, the one effect that is clearly significant is reranking. If I
    take it out, the mean reciprocal rank drops from 0.24 down to 0.10,
    with a p-value of about 0.013. So a good part of the retrieval benefit
    comes from combining the latent-space retrieval with rescoring by the
    forward model.

    And third --- this is the honest part --- the fragment branch and the
    derivation graph don't give me a statistically significant gain yet.
    In the form they're in right now, the fragment representation is
    largely redundant once you have the molecular encoder.

    So the main contribution here isn't a headline number from fragment
    augmentation. It's that there now \emph{is} a fragment-augmented
    bidirectional framework whose strengths and failure modes you can
    analyse systematically. And that's exactly what leads into the
    outlook.
}


\section{Conclusions}
\begin{frame}{Conclusions}
    \textbf{Contributions}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item One bidirectional model: M\O D graphs + two-level molecule
              + aligned spectral/structural embeddings
              ($f: M \!\to\! S$, $f^{-1}: S \!\to\! M$)
        \item Rule-extension DSL: alkyl / heteroatom / saturation
        \item Coupled objective: cosine\,+\,Wasserstein $\cdot$ InfoNCE
              $\cdot$ retrieval\,+\,rerank
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip 0.3em
    \textbf{Honest assessment}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Framework works; reranking helps
        \item Fragment branch: no significant gain yet
        \item Contribution = analysable framework, not accuracy win
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\note{
    So, to wrap up. First the contributions: as far as I know, this is the
    first framework that couples M\O D derivation graphs with a two-level
    molecular representation, and that aligns spectral and structural
    embeddings in one single bidirectional model --- so both the forward
    and the inverse direction. And along the way I built the
    rule-extension DSL with the chemical predicates I showed you, plus a
    coupled forward--backward training objective.

    And then the honest assessment: the framework works, reranking clearly
    helps, but the fragment branch doesn't beat the molecular encoder on
    its own yet. So what this work really gives you is a bidirectional
    framework whose behaviour and failure modes I can actually analyse ---
    and that's the setup for the outlook.
}

\begin{frame}{Outlook -- Learn to \emph{Generate} Fragmentation}
    Model \emph{encodes} precomputed M\O D graphs --- it does \emph{not}
    learn fragmentation.
    \vskip 0.5em
    Inspiration: \textbf{ICEBERG} \cite{Goldman2024} ---
    \emph{Generate}\,--\,\emph{Score}.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Have: the \textbf{Score} side
        \item Missing: a \textbf{Generate} module (which fragmentations?)
        \item M\O D graphs $\rightarrow$ proxy supervision
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip 0.5em
    {\footnotesize prune/rank $\to$ autoregressive expansion $\to$ joint
    end-to-end}
\end{frame}
\note{
    The clearest limit of what I've shown you is this: the model doesn't
    learn fragmentation. It encodes derivation graphs that the rule-based
    M\O D pipeline has already computed upstream. The learning problem only
    starts once the fragmentation space is built.

    So the natural next step --- and that's where I'd like to take this ---
    is to move from encoding fragmentation to generating it. The main
    inspiration here is ICEBERG, by Goldman and colleagues. They split the
    task in two: a Generate module that proposes likely fragmentation
    events, and a Score module that assigns the intensities.

    And if you look at my model through that lens, I already have the
    Score side. It embeds molecules, fragments and spectra into a shared
    space, it predicts spectra, it aligns and retrieves. What's missing is
    the Generate module upstream --- the part that decides which fragments
    even enter the picture. And the symbolic M\O D derivation graphs could
    serve as proxy supervision for training exactly that.

    I'd do it in stages: first learn to prune or rank derivation steps,
    then learn to expand the fragmentation graph autoregressively, and
    finally couple generation and scoring into one end-to-end model.
}

\begin{frame}{Outlook -- Toward a Generate--Score Architecture}
    \centering
    \begin{adjustbox}{max totalsize={\textwidth}{0.86\textheight},center}
        \input{../shared/figures/generative-future.tikz.tex}
    \end{adjustbox}
\end{frame}
\note{
    And this is roughly what that would look like.

    A learned Generate module, trained with the M\O D derivation graphs as
    proxy labels, proposes the fragmentation structure. And the Score
    module --- that's essentially my current model --- embeds the
    molecule, the fragments and the spectrum into a shared latent space,
    and produces both the forward spectrum prediction and the inverse
    retrieval.

    The two would be trained end-to-end. So the model wouldn't just learn
    how to \emph{use} fragments --- it would learn which fragmentation
    structures to build in the first place.
}
