\section{Problem}

\begin{frame}{What is my thesis about?}
    M \ldots Molecular structure \quad
    S \ldots Mass spectrum
    \begin{itemize}
%      \item \textbf{Computational mass spectrometry} with a focus on \textbf{MS spectrum modeling}
        \item Formulated mathematically as a function:
            \[
                f : M \to S
            \]
        \item Investigation of the \textbf{inverse problem}:
            \[
                f^{-1} : S \to M
            \]
        \item Integration of \textbf{chemically studied reaction rules} modeled in \textbf{M\O D}
        \item Use of \textbf{derivation graphs} (rule-level fragmentation
              relations) to support machine-learning-based MS models
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\note{
    So what is this project actually about?

    The starting point is quite simple. I treat mass-spectrometry
    modelling as a function: you put in a molecular structure, and you
    get out a mass spectrum. That's the forward direction.

    What I'm really after, though, is the inverse problem --- you have a
    spectrum, and you want to get back to the structure.

    And the idea I'm exploring is to bring the relationships between the
    fragments into that.

    I model those with M\O D, and then use them to help the
    machine-learning models along.
}

\section{Background}

\begin{frame}{Motivation}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{
        \input{../shared/figures/motivation.tikz.tex}
    }
    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\footnotesize Acquisition fast, interpretation slow $\Rightarrow$ the
    bottleneck.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    But why bother modelling this at all?
    What's the motivation?

    Mass spectrometry is one of the most widely used tools we have for
    analysing molecules. But reading that data is still mostly done by hand, by experts, and it takes time.

    So we can acquire spectra very fast --- and none of that speed
    carries over into the analysis. That's where the bottleneck sits.

    And the hope is: if we feed chemical structure into the models, they
    get more interpretable and they generalise better.
}

\note{
    A quick reminder of how a mass spectrometer works.

    We start with a sample, and that sample gets ionised.

    The ions are then separated by their mass-to-charge ratio,

    and at the end they hit a detector
    --- and that's what gives us the spectrum.

    The part that matters for us:
    under electron ionisation the molecule doesn't stay in one piece, it breaks apart.
    So the spectrum is really a fingerprint of those fragments.
    And that's exactly what I exploit later on.
}

\begin{frame}{Data Generation Overview}

    \resizebox{0.75\textwidth}{!}{

\note{
    This is the whole pipeline in one picture.

    I take a molecule and fragment it with the rules in M\O D.

    That gives me a fragmentation graph,

    and from that graph I read off the fragments.

    Then I go the other way round: I take those fragments and put them

    And that's how I get candidate structures for a given spectrum.
}

\note{
    At the top you see one of these rules the way it's written in the
    literature: equation 4.9, radical-induced alpha-cleavage at a saturated
    site. The radical cation sits on the heteroatom Y, a bond to the
    neighbouring carbon breaks, and a new pi bond forms between that carbon
    and Y.

    And the thing to notice is that the book doesn't only talk about single
    atoms --- it also refers to whole groups. Those are the R and Y
    placeholders.

    At the bottom is the same rule in code. The graph transformation itself
    only sees single atoms, so I carry the group conditions in the rule name,
    after that paragraph sign: here atoms 1, 3 and 4 have to be alkyl groups
    and atom 5 a heteroatom.

    And that extended notation is what the predicates on the next slides
    enforce during the transformation.
}


\begin{frame}{M\O D - Predicate}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-filter.tikz.tex}

\end{frame}
\note{
    M\O D lets you hook in so-called predicates. They're callbacks that
    get called during the transformation,

    and I use them to filter candidates according to the rule extension.

    On the next few slides I'll walk you through the ones I implemented.
}

\begin{frame}{M\O D - Predicate - Alkyl Group}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{%
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-alkyl.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    The first one is the alkyl group. An alkyl substituent contains
    nothing but carbon and hydrogen.

    So I start where the rule is extended, and collect the neighbours
    breadth-first, until I run into atoms that are already part of the
    match of the original rule.

    And then I simply check: is everything I collected a carbon or a
    hydrogen? If yes, it's a valid alkyl group. If not, I reject it.
}

\begin{frame}{M\O D - Predicate - Heteroatom Group}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{%
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-heteroatom.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    The heteroatom group is basically the mirror image of that. Here I
    need at least one atom that is \emph{not} a carbon or a hydrogen.

    Again I collect the neighbours breadth-first --- the ones the original
    rule hasn't matched ---

    and then I check whether that set contains anything outside hydrogen
    and carbon.

    If it does, it's a valid heteroatom group. If it's only hydrogen and
    carbon, it gets rejected.
}

\begin{frame}{M\O D - Predicate - Saturation}
    \vspace{1.5em}
    \centering
    \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-saturation.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    And the third one is saturation. Here I'm looking for a chain of
    carbons that are connected only by single bonds, and where every
    carbon carries nothing but hydrogens on the side.

    So I search from the start atom to the end atom, along carbon--carbon
    single bonds. The example at the top passes. The one at the bottom
    fails, and there are two reasons for that: the branching carbon, and
    the double bond.
}

\note{
    This is the model I implemented. It takes three inputs: the molecule
    graph, the derivation graph --- so the fragmentation --- and the mass
    spectrum.

    Each of them gets its own encoder, and they all map into one shared
    latent space. For the molecule that's a GCN with global pooling, for
    the derivation graph a two-level graph encoder, and for the spectrum a
    residual MLP.

    Going forward, I fuse the molecule and the fragment latents, and
    decode them into a predicted spectrum.

    The backward direction deserves a comment, because it is \emph{not} a
    spectrum-to-graph decoder. It's a retrieval path. I align the spectrum
    embedding with the molecular embeddings, so at test time I can pull
    out the nearest candidate structures by cosine similarity, and then
    rerank them with the forward model.

    Both directions share the task heads and the decoder, and they're tied
    together by two auxiliary losses: cycle consistency, and an InfoNCE
    contrastive alignment. So forward prediction and inverse retrieval end
    up constraining the very same latent space.
}

\note{
    And this is what inference looks like. I take a spectrum, and I embed
    it into the latent space.

    Then I retrieve the nearest molecular candidates by cosine similarity,
    and rerank them with the forward model.

    One caveat I want to put on the record here, because it comes up in the
    results: the reranking step does improve the raw retrieval numbers, but
    when I audited it, that improvement is the precursor-mass consistency
    filter inside it --- swapping the predicted spectrum for a constant one
    reproduces the same gain. So I do not claim the learned spectral shape is
    what drives retrieval.
}

\section{Results}
\begin{frame}{Results -- Mass Sets the Bar}
    {\footnotesize Held-out test split $\cdot$ $N = 13$ queries $\cdot$
    gallery of $66$ EI compounds}
    \vskip 0.5em
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{ l r }
      \hline
      Ranking signal & MRR \\
      \hline
      True parent mass (oracle)      & $0.923$ \\
      Spectrum-implied parent mass   & $0.462$ \\
      Mass mask $+$ cosine           & $0.498$ \\
      Learned embedding              & $0.112$ \\
      \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \vskip 0.6em
    {\footnotesize $\Rightarrow$ uncontrolled retrieval scores measure
    \textbf{mass agreement}, not chemistry --- so they are not the axis.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Now to the results, and I want to start with the thing that decides how
    every other number here has to be read.

    In this retrieval setting, parent mass alone is almost a solution. An
    oracle that ranks candidates by their true mass reaches a mean reciprocal
    rank of 0.92. Even a mass estimate read straight off the spectrum's
    heaviest peak gets 0.46.

    My learned embedding, scored on the full set, gets 0.11.

    So any raw retrieval score I could put on a slide sits under a very strong
    mass baseline --- including the apparent benefit of forward-model
    reranking, which is really the mass-consistency mask doing the work: mask
    plus cosine lands at 0.50, right at the mass baseline.

    That is why the honest question is not "how high is the retrieval score",
    but "is there anything left once mass is controlled for". Which is the
    next slide.
}

\begin{frame}{Results -- Mass-Controlled Retrieval}
    \centering
    \figorpending{width=0.72\linewidth}{../shared/figures/mass_controlled_mrr.pdf}
    \vskip 0.2em
    {\footnotesize
    $\pm5$\,Da: $0.551$ vs.\ chance $0.503$ \quad
    ($\Delta\,{+}0.048$, mean pool $5.4$) \\
    $\pm20$\,Da: $0.219$ vs.\ chance $0.206$ \quad
    ($\Delta\,{+}0.013$, mean pool $18.8$) \\[0.3em]
    Inside a window mass is ${\approx}$constant $\Rightarrow$ the margin is
    structure the mass cannot explain.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Here mass is held fixed. I take a window around the query's true mass ---
    plus/minus 5 Dalton, and plus/minus 20 --- and rank only the candidates
    inside it. Within such a window every candidate has essentially the same
    mass, so mass can no longer do the ranking for me.

    Against the analytic random-chance level for a pool of that size, the
    learned retrieval comes out at 0.551 versus 0.503 in the tight window, and
    0.219 versus 0.206 in the wide one. So a margin of about five and one
    percentage points.

    These are small margins, on thirteen held-out queries, from a single
    training run --- I am not going to oversell them. But they are positive on
    molecules that were never seen in training or model selection, and they
    are the only part of the evaluation that mass cannot account for.
}

\begin{frame}{Results -- The Forward Model Is Molecule-Specific}
    \centering
    \begin{adjustbox}{max totalsize={\textwidth}{0.62\textheight},center}
        \input{../shared/figures/recon_benzene.tikz.tex}
    \end{adjustbox}
    \vskip 0.2em
    {\footnotesize Predicted peaks land on \emph{this} molecule's fragment
    masses: $78\,[\mathrm{M}]^{+\bullet}$, $77\,[\mathrm{M-H}]^{+}$,
    $51\,\mathrm{C_4H_3^+}$, $26\,\mathrm{C_2H_2^+}$ --- the decoder
    \textbf{cannot} collapse to the dataset mean.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    And this is the property the mass-controlled result rests on.

    A free-form spectrum decoder has an easy failure mode: it predicts roughly
    the dataset-average spectrum for every input, and a reranker on top of it
    still looks like it is helping --- because what is helping is the mass
    filter, not the spectrum.

    My decoder cannot do that, by construction. Every predicted intensity is
    scattered from the molecule's own M\O D fragments, so the prediction is an
    explicit function of that molecule's fragment set. For benzene here the
    peaks fall at 78, the molecular ion, 77 for hydrogen loss, 51 for C4H3
    plus, and 26 for C2H2 plus --- all genuine fragments of the structure, not
    arbitrary bins.

    That is what the fragment branch demonstrably buys me: not a retrieval
    win, but a forward model that is molecule-specific by design.
}


\section{Conclusions}
\begin{frame}{Conclusions}
    \textbf{Contributions}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Fragment-grounded forward model}: intensities scattered
              from the molecule's own M\O D fragments $\Rightarrow$
              molecule-specific \emph{by construction}
        \item \textbf{Mass-controlled evaluation}: separates learned structure
              from parent-mass filtering
        \item Bidirectional framework ($f: M \!\to\! S$,
              $f^{-1}: S \!\to\! M$) + rule-extension DSL
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip 0.2em
    \textbf{Honest assessment}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Beyond-mass margin is \emph{small}: ${+}0.048$ / ${+}0.013$ MRR
              on $13$ queries, single run
        \item Uncontrolled metrics --- reranking included --- are mass
              filtering, not evidence
        \item Fragment branch buys molecule-specificity, not a retrieval win
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\note{
    So, to wrap up. Two contributions I would defend.

    First, the forward model is grounded in fragments: every predicted
    intensity is scattered from the molecule's own M\O D fragments, so it is
    molecule-specific by construction --- a design property, not a lucky
    result.

    Second, and more important: the evaluation. Mass is such a strong signal
    here that most retrieval numbers are mass filtering in disguise. The
    mass-controlled protocol separates the two.

    And around those, the bidirectional framework and the rule-extension DSL.

    Honestly: what survives the control is small --- plus five and plus one
    points of MRR, thirteen queries, one run. The uncontrolled metrics,
    reranking included, are mass consistency and I don't present them as
    evidence. The fragment branch earns its place through molecule-specificity,
    not a retrieval win. Which is the setup for the outlook.
}

\begin{frame}{Outlook -- Learn to \emph{Generate} Fragmentation}
    Model \emph{encodes} precomputed M\O D graphs --- it does \emph{not}
    learn fragmentation.
    \vskip 0.5em
    Inspiration: \textbf{ICEBERG} \cite{Goldman2024} ---
    \emph{Generate}\,--\,\emph{Score}.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Have: the \textbf{Score} side
        \item Missing: a \textbf{Generate} module (which fragmentations?)
        \item M\O D graphs $\rightarrow$ proxy supervision
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip 0.5em
    {\footnotesize prune/rank $\to$ autoregressive expansion $\to$ joint
    end-to-end}
\end{frame}
\note{
    The clearest limit of what I've shown you is this: the model doesn't
    learn fragmentation. It encodes derivation graphs that the rule-based
    M\O D pipeline has already computed upstream. The learning problem only
    starts once the fragmentation space is built.

    So the natural next step --- and that's where I'd like to take this ---
    is to move from encoding fragmentation to generating it. The main
    inspiration here is ICEBERG, by Goldman and colleagues. They split the
    task in two: a Generate module that proposes likely fragmentation
    events, and a Score module that assigns the intensities.

    And if you look at my model through that lens, I already have the
    Score side. It embeds molecules, fragments and spectra into a shared
    space, it predicts spectra, it aligns and retrieves. What's missing is
    the Generate module upstream --- the part that decides which fragments
    even enter the picture. And the symbolic M\O D derivation graphs could
    serve as proxy supervision for training exactly that.

    I'd do it in stages: first learn to prune or rank derivation steps,
    then learn to expand the fragmentation graph autoregressively, and
    finally couple generation and scoring into one end-to-end model.
}

\begin{frame}{Outlook -- Toward a Generate--Score Architecture}
    \centering
    \begin{adjustbox}{max totalsize={\textwidth}{0.86\textheight},center}
        \reportmetrics\input{../shared/figures/generative-future.tikz.tex}
    \end{adjustbox}
\end{frame}
\note{
    And this is roughly what that would look like.

    A learned Generate module, trained with the M\O D derivation graphs as
    proxy labels, proposes the fragmentation structure. And the Score
    module --- that's essentially my current model --- embeds the
    molecule, the fragments and the spectrum into a shared latent space,
    and produces both the forward spectrum prediction and the inverse
    retrieval.

    The two would be trained end-to-end. So the model wouldn't just learn
    how to \emph{use} fragments --- it would learn which fragmentation
    structures to build in the first place.
}

\subsection{Spectral Comparison Measures}

\begin{frame}{Cosine Similarity -- Geometry}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/cosine_vectors.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize $\cos\theta = (A\cdot B)/(\|A\|\|B\|)$ --- direction only,
    hence scale-invariant.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Cosine similarity is just the angle between the two intensity vectors. It
    depends on direction only, so rescaling a spectrum doesn't change it.
}

\begin{frame}{Wasserstein Distance -- Illustration}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/wasserstein_expl.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Sorted vectors as cumulative staircases; the distance is
    the shaded transport area, normalised by $n$.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    The Wasserstein distance as transport cost: draw both sorted vectors as
    cumulative staircases, and the shaded area between them --- divided by
    the number of elements --- is the distance.
}

\begin{frame}{Wasserstein Distance -- Upsampling}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/wasserstein_upsampling.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Unequal peak counts are brought to a common length
    $\mathrm{LCM}(n,m)$ by repetition, preserving each value's share.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    The matched-pair form needs both vectors to have the same length, and
    real spectra never do. So each is repeated up to the least common
    multiple, which keeps every value's relative share of the total mass.
}

\begin{frame}{Cosine vs.\ Wasserstein -- (a) Aligned Spectra}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/cosine_vs_wasserstein_a.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Every B peak meets its A counterpart: cosine $=1$
    ($\theta=0$), Wasserstein $=0$ (no mass to move).}
\end{frame}
\note{
    The reference case: spectrum A upward, spectrum B mirrored downward,
    peaks aligned. Both measures agree that these are identical.
}

\begin{frame}{Cosine vs.\ Wasserstein -- (b) B Shifted $+8$\,Da}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/cosine_vs_wasserstein_b.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Cosine counts only \emph{shared} positions $\Rightarrow$
    collapses to $0$; Wasserstein transports the mass back and stays finite
    ($21.1$).}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Now shift B by 8 Dalton. Cosine only sees intensity at shared positions,
    so its dot product vanishes and it drops straight to zero --- even though
    the spectra are obviously still related. Wasserstein measures the work of
    moving B's mass back onto A and returns a finite number.
}

\begin{frame}{Cosine vs.\ Wasserstein -- (c) Response vs.\ Shift}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/cosine_vs_wasserstein_c.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Cosine drops to zero at once, with spurious spikes at
    ${\sim}14$ and $17$\,Da; Wasserstein rises linearly with displacement.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Sweeping the shift makes it systematic. Cosine falls off a cliff and even
    shows spurious spikes, where a displaced peak accidentally lands on a
    different peak. Wasserstein degrades gracefully, in proportion to the
    displacement.
}

\begin{frame}{Cosine vs.\ Wasserstein -- (d) Response vs.\ Scaling}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/cosine_vs_wasserstein_d.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Cosine unchanged at $1.00$ (direction, not magnitude);
    un-normalized Wasserstein grows with the added intensity.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    And the complementary axis: scale B's intensities with the positions held
    fixed. Cosine doesn't move, because it compares direction, not magnitude.
    The un-normalized Wasserstein distance grows with the added mass.

    So the two measures capture different things --- pattern at fixed
    coordinates versus position along the axis --- which is why the loss uses
    both.
}
