\section{Problem}

\begin{frame}{Motivation}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{
        \input{../shared/figures/motivation.tikz.tex}
    }
    \vskip 0.6cm

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Mass spectrometry is widely used, but analysis is the challenge
        \item Data acquisition is fast, interpretation slow $\Rightarrow$ the bottleneck
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}
\note{
    What is the motivation?

    Mass spectrometry is among the most widely used techniques for analyzing molecules.
    Interpretation of the data for novel molecules, however, is still largely manual and expert-driven, and correspondingly slow.

    Data acquisition is fast --- and none of that speed carries over into the analysis.
    That asymmetry is where the bottleneck sits.
    Therefore, developing computational methods in structure elucidation is a worthwhile goal.
}

\begin{frame}{Problem Statement}
    % defenition
    \small{
    $\mathcal{M}$ \ldots Space of molecular structures\\
    $\mathcal{S}$ \ldots Space of mass spectra\\
    }
    Stated formally:
    \begin{itemize}
%      \item \textbf{Computational mass spectrometry} with a focus on \textbf{MS spectrum modeling}
        \item The \textbf{forward problem}:
            \[
                f : \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{S}
            \]
        \item The inverse problem, known as \textbf{structure elucidation},
            is the ultimate goal:
            \[
                f^{-1} : \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{M}
            \]
        \item Difficult, because spectra are near-identical for many molecules:
            \[ d(f(m), s) < \varepsilon \quad
            \text{\footnotesize holds for many } m \in \mathcal{M} \]

    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Stated formally, the problem is to model the mapping from molecular structures to mass spectra, and vice versa.

    Under fixed measurement conditions, we have a function mapping one structure to one spectrum.

    And what I am ultimately after is the inverse problem, so-called structure elucidation.
    Given a spectrum, recover the structure.

    But this problem is hard.
    Spectra of different molecules can lie very close together.
    So what matches a given spectrum, within epsilon, is not one molecule. It is a large set.
}

\section{Background}

\begin{frame}{Mass Spectrometer}
    \centering
    \begin{adjustbox}{max totalsize={0.95\textwidth}{0.74\textheight},center}
        \input{../shared/figures/ms-schematic.tikz.tex}
    \end{adjustbox}
\end{frame}
\note{
    A brief reminder of the measurement principle.

    We start with a sample, which is first ionized and accellerated into the mass analyzer.

    There the ions are then separated according to their mass-to-charge ratio, and are finally registered at a detector --- which is what yields the spectrum.

    The point that matters for this work:
    under electron ionization the molecule does not remain intact, but fragments.
    The spectrum is therefore a fingerprint of those fragments, and it is precisely that structure I exploit later on.
    So the spectrum can be related to a certain molecular structure.
}

\begin{frame}{Data Generation Overview}

    \resizebox{0.75\textwidth}{!}{
        \import{../shared/figures/}{dataGenOverview.tikz.tex}

\note{
    This figure summarizes the entire pipeline of the synthetic data generation.

    A molecule is fragmented by applying the rule set in a Graph Transformation Tool.

    That yields a fragmentation graph, from which the fragments are read off.

    The reverse direction then \textbf{recombines} those fragments into theoretically possible structures, using the inverted reaction rules.

    This is how candidate structures for a given spectrum are obtained.
}

\note{
    At the top you see one of these rules in its original, literature form.

    The essential observation is this.
    The formulation is not restricted to individual atoms.
    It also quantifies over entire substituent groups.
    Those are the R and Y placeholders, for alkyl and heteroatom groups.

    Below is the same rule in code.
    The graph transformation itself operates on single atoms only.
    So I carry the group conditions in the rule name, after the paragraph sign.
    Here atoms 1, 3 and 4 are required to be alkyl groups, and atom 5 a heteroatom group.

    On the following slides you will see how this notation is enforced during the transformation.
}


\begin{frame}{Predicate}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-filter.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    M\O D, the Graph Transformation Tool used for this project, provides an interface for so-called predicates.
    Predicates are callbacks that are evaluated during the transformation.
    They are here used to filter candidate matches according to the rule extension.
    The next few slides discuss the ones I implemented.
}

\begin{frame}{Predicate -- Alkyl Group}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{%
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-alkyl.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    The first is the alkyl group.
    An alkyl substituent consists of nothing but carbon and hydrogen.

    I start at the atom at which the rule is defined.
    From there I collect the neighborhood breadth-first.
    The search terminates at atoms that already belong to the match of the original rule.

    The condition is then simply whether every collected atom is a carbon or a hydrogen.
    If so, the group is accepted as alkyl; otherwise the match is rejected.
}

\begin{frame}{Predicate -- Heteroatom Group}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{%
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-heteroatom.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    The heteroatom group is the complementary condition.
    Here at least one atom is required to be \emph{neither} carbon \emph{nor} hydrogen.

    Again I collect the neighborhood breadth-first.
    Only atoms the original rule has not matched are considered.

    The set is then tested for any element outside hydrogen and carbon.

    If such an atom is present, the group is accepted.
    If the set is hydrogen and carbon only, the match is rejected.
}

\begin{frame}{Predicate -- Saturation}
    \vspace{1.5em}
    \centering
    \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{
        \input{../shared/figures/predicate-saturation.tikz.tex}
    }
\end{frame}
\note{
    The third predicate is saturation.
    The requirement is a chain of carbon atoms connected exclusively by single bonds, in which every carbon carries hydrogen substituents only.

    The search proceeds from the start atom to the end atom, along carbon--carbon single bonds.
    The upper example satisfies the condition.
    The lower example does not, because of the branched carbon.
}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Architecture -- Training}
    \centering
    \IfFileExists{../shared/figures/ms_architecture_training.tex}{%
        \begin{adjustbox}{max totalsize={\textwidth}{0.86\textheight},center}
            \input{../shared/figures/ms_architecture_training.tex}
        \end{adjustbox}%

            \rule{0pt}{0.3\textheight}%
            \ph{[figure pending]}%
        }%

\note{
    \scriptsize
    Now to the machine-learning model.
    This is the architecture, trained in two phases --- Phase A and Phase B.

    Three things go in: the measured spectrum, the molecule graph and the fragmentation graph.
    Each has its own encoder, and each produces a vector of the same dimension.
    A GINEConv network with pooling encodes the molecule.
    A two-level encoder handles the fragmentation graph: first each fragment, then a GCN over the whole graph.
    A residual MLP encodes the spectrum.

    Phase A is the forward direction.
    The molecule and fragment latents are averaged, passed through the forward head, and used to condition the decoder.

    The decoder is the part worth highlighting.
    It does not emit a free-form spectrum.
    It predicts one intensity per fragment and scatters it onto that fragment's m/z bin --- which is why the per-fragment embeddings and masses come in from the right.
    The result is masked by the parent mass, then scored with cosine plus Wasserstein: the dotted line down the left margin.

    Phase B is the backward direction, with its own encoder and head.
    The green arrow is the InfoNCE alignment, pulling each spectrum toward its own molecule and away from the others.
    That is what makes retrieval possible --- the next slide.
}

    \centering
    \IfFileExists{../shared/figures/ms_architecture_inference.tex}{%

            \input{../shared/figures/ms_architecture_inference.tex}
        \end{adjustbox}%
    }{%
        \framebox[0.8\linewidth]{%
            \rule{0pt}{0.3\textheight}%
            \ph{[figure pending]}%
        }%

\note{
    \scriptsize
    At inference only the amber column is live.
    We have a spectrum and want the structure, so nothing on the molecule side is available as input any more.

    The spectrum takes the same backward path as in training --- binned spectrum, residual MLP, backward head --- and gives one vector in the shared latent space.

    The molecule latents for the whole candidate library are precomputed and normalized in advance.
    A query is then a nearest-neighbour lookup that returns the top-$k$ molecules.

    The second stage is the rerank: for each candidate we run the forward model, predict its spectrum, and score it against the spectrum we measured --- the dotted line back to the input.
    Retrieval proposes, the forward model checks.

    Keep the two stages apart, because the results will separate them.
    The rerank also carries a parent-mass mask, and that mask turns out to do most of the work.
}

\section{Results}
\begin{frame}{Results -- Mass Sets the Bar}
    \footnotesize{ Held-out test split $\cdot$ $N = 13$ queries $\cdot$ gallery of $66$ EI compounds}
    \vskip 2em
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{ l r }
      \hline
      Ranking signal & MRR \\
      \hline
      True parent mass (oracle)      & $0.923$ \\
      Spectrum-implied parent mass   & $0.462$ \\
      Mass mask $+$ cosine           & $0.498$ \\
      Learned embedding              & $0.112$ \\
      \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \vskip 1.6em
    {\footnotesize $\Rightarrow$ uncontrolled retrieval scores measure
    \textbf{mass agreement}, not chemistry}
\end{frame}
\note{
    I now turn to the results.
    I start with the observation that determines how every other number has to be read.

    With a gallery of only 66 compounds, parent mass almost identifies the molecule on its own.
    An oracle that ranks candidates by their true mass attains a mean reciprocal rank of 0.92.
    Even a mass estimate read off the heaviest peak attains 0.46.

    The learned embedding, scored on the full gallery, attains 0.11.

    So any raw retrieval figure sits below a very strong mass baseline.
    That includes the apparent benefit of forward-model reranking.
    What helps there is the mass-consistency mask.
    Mask plus cosine yields 0.50 --- the mass baseline itself.

    So the defensible question is not how high the retrieval score is.
    It is whether anything remains once mass is controlled for.
    That is the next slide.
}

\begin{frame}{Results -- Mass-Controlled Retrieval}
    \centering
    \figorpending{width=0.72\linewidth}{../shared/figures/mass_controlled_mrr.pdf}
    \vskip 0.2em
    {\footnotesize
    $\pm5$\,Da: $0.551$ vs.\ chance $0.503$ \quad
    ($\Delta\,{+}0.048$, mean pool $5.4$) \\
    $\pm20$\,Da: $0.219$ vs.\ chance $0.206$ \quad
    ($\Delta\,{+}0.013$, mean pool $18.8$) \\[0.3em]
    Inside a window mass is ${\approx}$constant $\Rightarrow$ the margin is
    structure the mass cannot explain.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Here mass is held fixed.
    I restrict the ranking to candidates within a window around the query's true mass.
    Plus or minus 5 Dalton, and plus or minus 20.
    Within such a window every candidate has essentially the same mass.
    So mass can no longer account for the ranking.

    I measure against the analytic chance level for a pool of that size.
    In the narrow window the learned retrieval attains 0.551, against 0.503.
    In the wide window, 0.219 against 0.206.
    That is a margin of roughly five and one percentage points.

    They are positive on molecules seen neither in training nor in model selection.
    And they are the only part of the evaluation that mass cannot explain.
}

\begin{frame}{Results -- The Forward Model Is Molecule-Specific}
    \centering
    \begin{adjustbox}{max totalsize={\textwidth}{0.62\textheight},center}
        \input{../shared/figures/recon_benzene.tikz.tex}
    \end{adjustbox}
    \vskip 0.2em
    {\footnotesize Predicted peaks land on \emph{this} molecule's \emph{derived}
    fragment masses: $78\,[\mathrm{M}]^{+\bullet}$, $77\,[\mathrm{M-H}]^{+}$,
    $51\,\mathrm{C_4H_3^+}$ (all measured); $26\,\mathrm{C_2H_2^+}$ derived but
    \emph{not} observed --- the decoder \textbf{cannot} collapse to the dataset mean.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    One more result, and this one concerns the forward model rather than retrieval.

    Unconstrained spectrum decoders have a well-known failure mode.
    Such a decoder predicts roughly the dataset-average spectrum for every input.
    A reranker placed on top appears to help, but the help comes from the mass filter, not from the spectrum.

    My decoder cannot produce a dataset-average spectrum, by construction.
    Every predicted intensity is scattered from fragments derived for the input molecule.
    The prediction is therefore an explicit function of the input molecule's fragment set.

    The figure shows the spectrum of benzene.
    The measured spectrum points upward, the prediction is mirrored downward.
    Predicted peaks fall at 78, the molecular ion, 77 for hydrogen loss, and 51 for a fragment.
    All three appear in the measured spectrum.
    A fourth predicted peak sits at 26, which the rules derive but the measurement does not show.
    Vice versa, the measured spectrum has a peak at 39, which the rules do not derive and the model does not predict.

    So: every predicted mass comes from the input molecule's own fragment set.
    A decoder built that way cannot fall back on the dataset mean.

    The fragment branch buys molecule-specificity by design, not a retrieval gain.
}


\section{Conclusions}
\begin{frame}{Conclusions}
    \textbf{Contributions}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Fragment-grounded forward model}: intensities scattered
              from the molecule's own derived fragments $\Rightarrow$
              molecule-specific \emph{by construction}
        \item \textbf{Mass-controlled evaluation}: separates learned structure
              from parent-mass filtering
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip 1em
    {\footnotesize \textbf{Supporting work}: bidirectional framework
    ($f: \mathcal{M} \!\to\! \mathcal{S}$, $f^{-1}: \mathcal{S} \!\to\! \mathcal{M}$)
    $+$ rule-extension DSL}

\end{frame}
\note{
    To summarize.
    There are two contributions I would defend.

    First, the forward model is fragment-grounded.
    Every predicted intensity is scattered from the molecule's own derived fragments.
    So it is molecule-specific by construction --- a design property, not an empirical accident.

    Second, and more importantly, the evaluation.
    Mass is so strong a signal here that most retrieval figures amount to mass filtering.
    The mass-controlled protocol separates the two.

    Around those sit the bidirectional framework and the rule-extension DSL.
}

\begin{frame}{Outlook -- Learn to \emph{Generate} Fragmentation}
        \centering
        \begin{adjustbox}{max totalsize={\linewidth}{0.78\textheight},center}
            \reportmetrics\input{../shared/figures/generative-future.tikz.tex}
        \end{adjustbox}
        % ICEBERG is credited in the spoken note, not on the slide; \nocite
        % keeps it in the bibliography without printing a mark here.
        \nocite{Goldman2024}
\end{frame}
\note{
    \small
    A structural design premise of this thesis: the model encodes precomputed fragmentation graphs.
    It does not learn fragmentation itself.

    The principal inspiration for this is ICEBERG, by Goldman and colleagues.
    It divides the task in two.
    A Generate module proposes likely fragmentation events.
    A Score module assigns the intensities.

    Viewed through that lens, my model already provides the Score side --- the lower half of the figure.
    It embeds molecules, fragments and spectra into a shared latent space, and produces both the forward prediction and the inverse retrieval.

    What is missing is the Generate module upstream, the component deciding which fragments enter the picture at all.
    The symbolic fragmentation graphs could serve as proxy supervision for training exactly that.

    The two would then be trained end-to-end.
    The model would learn not only how to use fragments, but which fragmentation structures to construct in the first place.
}

\begin{frame}{Electron Ionization (EI)}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/ei-schematic.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize ${\sim}70$\,eV electrons eject one electron
    $\Rightarrow$ radical cation $[M]^{+\bullet}$, which then fragments.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    This is the ionization method the entire thesis is concerned with. A
    70-electron-volt beam ejects one electron, producing a radical cation
    whose excess internal energy is what drives the fragmentation.
}

\begin{frame}{Electrospray Ionization (ESI)}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/esi-schematic.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Soft ionization: charged droplets $\rightarrow$ gas-phase
    ions, typically $[\mathrm{M+H}]^{+}$.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    ESI for contrast: a soft method, which largely preserves the intact
    molecule and produces very little fragmentation --- which is precisely
    the information my approach relies on.
}

\begin{frame}{Atmospheric Pressure Chemical Ionization (APCI)}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/apci-schematic.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Corona discharge makes reagent ions; proton transfer gives
    $[\mathrm{M+H}]^{+}$.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    APCI: the solvent is ionized first by a corona discharge, and the
    resulting reagent ions then transfer a proton to the analyte.
}

\begin{frame}{Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization (MALDI)}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/maldi-schematic.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Laser desorption from a matrix; predominantly singly
    charged ions, suited to large biomolecules.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    MALDI: the analyte is embedded in a matrix, a laser pulse desorbs both,
    and the matrix ions effect the ionization. The ions produced are
    predominantly singly charged, and the method scales to large biomolecules.
}

\begin{frame}{Electron Multiplier Detector}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/em-schematic.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Cascading secondary electrons across dynodes amplify a
    single ion into a measurable current pulse.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    And the detection side: a single ion strikes the first dynode, each
    stage multiplies the electrons, and after several stages the result is
    a current pulse that can be digitized.
}

\subsection{Spectral Comparison Measures}

\begin{frame}{Cosine Similarity -- Geometry}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/cosine_vectors.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize $\cos\theta = (A\cdot B)/(\|A\|\|B\|)$ --- direction only,
    hence scale-invariant.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Cosine similarity is the angle between the two intensity vectors. It
    depends on direction alone, so rescaling a spectrum leaves it unchanged.
}

\begin{frame}{Wasserstein Distance -- Illustration}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/wasserstein_expl.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Sorted vectors as cumulative staircases; the distance is
    the shaded transport area, normalized by $n$.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    The Wasserstein distance as a transport cost: both sorted vectors are
    drawn as cumulative staircases, and the shaded area between them ---
    divided by the number of elements --- is the distance.
}

\begin{frame}{Wasserstein Distance -- Upsampling}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/wasserstein_upsampling.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Unequal peak counts are brought to a common length
    $\mathrm{LCM}(n,m)$ by repetition, preserving each value's share.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    The matched-pair form requires both vectors to be of equal length, which
    real spectra never are. Each is therefore repeated up to the least common
    multiple, which preserves every value's relative share of the total mass.
}

\begin{frame}{Cosine vs.\ Wasserstein -- (a) Aligned Spectra}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/cosine_vs_wasserstein_a.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Every B peak meets its A counterpart: cosine $=1$
    ($\theta=0$), Wasserstein $=0$ (no mass to move).}
\end{frame}
\note{
    The reference case: spectrum A plotted upward, spectrum B mirrored
    downward, peaks aligned. Both measures agree that these are identical.
}

\begin{frame}{Cosine vs.\ Wasserstein -- (b) B Shifted $+8$\,Da}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/cosine_vs_wasserstein_b.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Cosine counts only \emph{shared} positions $\Rightarrow$
    collapses to $0$; Wasserstein transports the mass back and stays finite
    ($21.1$).}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Now shift B by 8 Dalton. Cosine registers intensity only at shared
    positions, so its dot product vanishes and it drops straight to zero ---
    although the spectra are evidently still related. Wasserstein measures the
    work required to transport B's mass back onto A, and returns a finite
    value.
}

\begin{frame}{Cosine vs.\ Wasserstein -- (c) Response vs.\ Shift}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/cosine_vs_wasserstein_c.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Cosine drops to zero at once, with spurious spikes at
    ${\sim}14$ and $17$\,Da; Wasserstein rises linearly with displacement.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Sweeping the shift makes this systematic. Cosine falls off abruptly and
    even exhibits spurious spikes, where a displaced peak coincides with a
    different peak. Wasserstein degrades gracefully, in proportion to the
    displacement.
}

\begin{frame}{Cosine vs.\ Wasserstein -- (d) Response vs.\ Scaling}
    \centering
    \suppfig{../shared/figures/cosine_vs_wasserstein_d.tikz.tex}
    \vskip 0.3em
    {\footnotesize Cosine unchanged at $1.00$ (direction, not magnitude);
    un-normalized Wasserstein grows with the added intensity.}
\end{frame}
\note{
    And the complementary axis: B's intensities are scaled with the positions
    held fixed. Cosine is unaffected, as it compares direction, not magnitude,
    whereas the un-normalized Wasserstein distance grows with the added mass.

    The two measures therefore capture different properties --- pattern at
    fixed coordinates versus position along the axis --- which is why the loss
    employs both.
}

\note{
    The figures underlying the results slides. 66 molecules had both a
    fragmentation graph and a measured spectrum, which splits 38 / 15 / 13.
    Retrieval is closed-library: the gallery comprises all 66, which is the
    structure-elucidation setting, and the 13 test molecules were seen neither
    in training nor in model selection.

    All results come from a single training run, without seed averaging. That
    is the principal reason I describe the work as a proof of concept.
}

\begin{frame}{Honest assessment}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Beyond-mass margin is \emph{small}: ${+}0.048$ / ${+}0.013$ MRR
              on $13$ queries, single run
        \item Uncontrolled metrics --- reranking included --- are mass
              filtering, not evidence
        \item Fragment branch buys molecule-specificity, not a retrieval win
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\note{
    Now the honest assessment.
    What survives the control is small.
    Roughly five and one percentage points of MRR, on thirteen queries and a single run.
    The uncontrolled metrics, reranking included, reflect mass consistency.
    I do not present them as evidence.
    The fragment branch earns its place through molecule-specificity, not through a retrieval gain.
    Which leads directly to the outlook.
}
